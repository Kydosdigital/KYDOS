Start with the business problem

CRO should improve the percentage of suitable visitors who complete a meaningful action. It is not simply redesigning buttons or making a page look cleaner. The agency needs evidence about where users struggle and a method for deciding what to change first.

A low-traffic business may not have enough volume for statistically useful A/B testing. CRO can still mean fixing obvious friction, clarifying the offer, improving forms and strengthening trust based on behavioural evidence.

Ask who is responsible for implementation

Ask the provider to separate measurement fixes, usability issues and true experiments. Every proposed change should have a reason, an expected behavioural effect and a way to check whether it helped.

Do not assume a strategy, audit or setup line automatically includes every practical change needed to make it live. Put dependencies and internal responsibilities into the proposal.

Agree how success will be measured

The headline conversion rate should be interpreted alongside lead quality, revenue or booking value where possible. More submissions are not automatically a win if they are worse prospects.

Decide the reporting model before delivery so both sides know which numbers are useful and which are merely diagnostic.

Protect ownership and handover

Keep appropriate business ownership of platforms, data and core accounts. Document administrator access, exports and what happens if the supplier relationship ends.

Ask for proof that matches the work

Ask for an example hypothesis backlog showing the evidence, proposed change, priority and metric for each item.

Where client information is confidential, a supplier can still demonstrate process quality through redacted documents, owned properties or implementation evidence without inventing outcomes.

Watch for this red flag

Red flag: The agency presents a long list of design opinions as a CRO audit without showing data, user evidence or a measurement plan.
