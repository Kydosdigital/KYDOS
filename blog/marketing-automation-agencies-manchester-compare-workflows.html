Start with the business problem

Marketing automation becomes valuable when it removes repeatable follow-up work without making the customer journey feel mechanical. The supplier should know what deserves automation and what still needs a person.

The safest automation design starts with a simple journey and clear stop conditions. More branches are not automatically better because every trigger and delay creates another path that needs testing.

Ask who is responsible for implementation

Ask the provider to document each workflow as trigger, conditions, actions, delays, stop rules and human hand-offs. Review this before messages are sent to real prospects.

Do not assume a strategy, audit or setup line automatically includes every practical change needed to make it live. Put dependencies and internal responsibilities into the proposal.

Agree how success will be measured

Do not measure automation by the number of workflows. Measure whether follow-up happens reliably, whether leads move through the correct stages and whether staff can see when human intervention is needed.

Decide the reporting model before delivery so both sides know which numbers are useful and which are merely diagnostic.

Protect ownership and handover

Keep appropriate business ownership of platforms, data and core accounts. Document administrator access, exports and what happens if the supplier relationship ends.

Ask for proof that matches the work

Ask to review the workflow logic in plain English and request test evidence for the main success and failure paths.

Where client information is confidential, a supplier can still demonstrate process quality through redacted documents, owned properties or implementation evidence without inventing outcomes.

Watch for this red flag

Red flag: The agency proposes a complex automation map without defining stop conditions, ownership or what happens when a person replies.
