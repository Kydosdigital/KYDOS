Google Search Console Shows Impressions but No Clicks: What Should You Check?

Find out whether your problem is ranking, the search result people see, or a website that does not turn visitors into enquiries.

Published 21 November 2026Seven practical checksGreater Manchester
Short answer: An impression means Google counted an appearance of your result, not that somebody seriously considered clicking it. If your page usually appears far down the results, improving relevance and visibility matters more than changing the meta description. If it already appears prominently for a relevant question, inspect the actual search results, title and competing features before changing the page. If people click but do not enquire, check the page and its tracking instead.

What do impressions, clicks, CTR and average position mean?

In Google Search Console, an impression records when a link to your site is shown according to Google's search-result reporting rules. A click records a click from Google Search to your property. CTR is clicks divided by impressions, expressed as a percentage. Average position summarises the position of your topmost result when impressions occurred. It is not a promise that everyone saw your page in the same position.

Illustrative calculation: 100 impressions and 2 clicks equals a 2% CTR. That number alone does not tell you whether the page is underperforming. A service page usually shown around position 24 and a relevant page usually shown around position 5 need different investigations. Search Console also has privacy-filtered query data, so totals across visible queries may not equal the page or site totals.

Seven checks before you rewrite your page

1. Confirm the right page and date range

Open Search Console, select Performance > Search results, choose a complete date range and filter to the exact page URL. Compare with a similar preceding period rather than today's partial figures. Make sure you are viewing Web results if you intend to analyse ordinary web search.

2. Look at the query and page together

Open the Queries tab for that page. Ask whether each search is actually a question the page answers. A Manchester service comparison page may appear for a generic informational search but not satisfy it. Separate searches containing your brand from non-branded service or problem questions; they reflect different reader needs. Search Console may hide some queries for privacy, so do not assume the visible list is complete.

3. Check average position before blaming the title

Sort the relevant queries by impressions and inspect position alongside clicks. If the page averages around position 20 or worse, the immediate issue is often discoverability or query relevance, not the snippet. If a query averages around positions 4 to 10 but earns no clicks, look at what people actually see in the results. These ranges are diagnostic starting points, not fixed ranking thresholds. Position varies by date, device and search location.

4. Inspect the actual search results and their features

Search a representative query on a relevant device and location, without treating your one test as a universal ranking report. Ads, local map packs, shopping results, featured snippets and AI features can change what a searcher sees. Check whether the result is truly relevant, whether competitors answer the question more clearly, and whether your page offers a reason to visit.

5. Review the visible title link and description, not just your HTML tags

Google can generate title links from the title element, visible headings and other page signals. It can also select snippets from page content instead of your meta description. Make the page title and opening answer clear, specific and consistent, but do not assume a metadata rewrite will instantly raise CTR. See Google's title-link guidance and snippet guidance.

6. Make sure the landing page fulfils the promise

Read the page on a phone as a first-time customer. Does it answer the query in the opening paragraphs? Is there a usable checklist, real explanation, price context or comparison? Can somebody find contact details without hunting? An article about fixing Google Maps visibility should not begin with a long sales pitch for an SEO retainer.

7. If clicks exist but leads do not, test conversion and measurement

Check mobile loading, the contact form, call links and what happens after submission. Verify that analytics events represent real successful enquiries rather than mere form views or button clicks. Do not report zero enquiries just because an analytics key event is not configured. If you have clicks but no enquiries, the problem may be customer fit, page usefulness, conversion friction or incomplete tracking.

How this might apply to a Greater Manchester business

Hypothetical Oldham tradesperson: Their service page appears for searches about a service in Oldham but sits far below the map pack. First check the page's service relevance, actual operating area and whether a separate eligible Business Profile is accurate. Do not assume adding 'Oldham' ten more times will help.

Hypothetical Stockport clinic: Its service page appears prominently but receives few clicks. Compare what the searcher sees, including the clinic's service explanation and competitors' result titles. Do not claim the clinic needs more advertising until the existing search opportunity has been assessed.

Hypothetical Manchester consultant: Their blog attracts visitors researching a problem but no quote requests. That article may be working as educational content. Look at whether it offers an appropriate next guide or service page, rather than forcing a sales CTA into every paragraph.

Questions business owners ask

Does an impression mean someone saw and read my result?

No. An impression follows Google's reporting rules for how a result appears. It is not proof that someone read your title, considered your business or wanted to visit.

Is a 0% CTR always bad?

No. A page with a low average position, irrelevant query impressions or a tiny sample can show zero clicks without revealing a title problem. Judge relevance, ranking and the search-results layout first.

Should I add Manchester, Oldham and Stockport to every title?

No. Use the town where it helps answer a real local question or describes a genuine service area. Repeating locations without useful distinctions can make a page less helpful.

Can I guarantee an AI Overview will link to my page?

No. Google controls which sources and features appear. Clear, original and reliable content can help people, but there is no guaranteed AI citation or click-through method.
