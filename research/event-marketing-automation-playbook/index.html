The Event Marketing Automation Playbook.

A practical system for turning event interest into registrations, attendance, follow-up and measurable outcomes.

Methodology

This is a practitioner playbook based on Kydos Digital event-marketing, CRM and automation work. It explains an operating model, not a statistical market benchmark. The framework synthesises patterns used in multi-location workshop and event systems and is intended to help teams define the lifecycle before they automate it.

Do not automate a journey you have not defined. Automation amplifies the quality of the underlying process, including its gaps.

1. Treat the event as a funnel, not a date in the diary.

The minimum useful event funnel has four stages. Each stage needs a clear entry rule, owner and record in the CRM. If the team cannot answer what state a contact is in without checking several tools, the event system is already too fragmented.

StageDefinitionUseful question
InterestA contact has engaged with the event proposition.Who is eligible for event marketing but has not registered?
RegistrationThe contact has completed the required booking or registration action.Who has made a measurable commitment to attend?
AttendanceThe registrant is marked attended or no-show.Did reminders and logistics translate registration into show-up?
OutcomeA defined next result after the event.Did the event produce feedback, booked calls, sales or another intended result?

Core conversion metrics

  • Interest to registration: registrations divided by interested contacts.
  • Registration to attendance: attendees divided by registrations.
  • Interest to attendance: attendees divided by interested contacts.
  • No-show rate: no-shows divided by registrations.
  • Attendance to outcome: defined outcomes divided by attendees.

2. Make registration easy to complete and easy to measure.

A campaign can be well targeted and still fail at the registration layer. The landing page should remove practical uncertainty: who the event is for, what happens, date, time, venue, travel information, proof, FAQs and one obvious registration action. The mobile journey matters because many registrations happen away from a desktop.

Eventbrite can be useful for discovery and ticket administration, but it should not automatically become a separate data silo. Website registrations, Eventbrite registrations and paid-media leads should reconcile into the same lifecycle model wherever technically practical.

3. Use two workflows, not one giant automation.

Separate event acquisition from attendee operations. The first workflow promotes the event to an eligible audience. The second begins only after registration. This prevents registered attendees from continuing to receive book-now messaging and keeps confirmations, reminders and post-event branches easier to manage.

4. Use a naming system that survives the next event.

CRM structure becomes difficult when every workshop uses a different naming convention. Standardise event type, location, lifecycle state and outcome so the same reporting model can be reused.

5. Every message should answer a different question.

Repeating the same event announcement seven times is not a sequence. A useful sequence reduces a different point of uncertainty or strengthens a different reason to act at each step.

Marketing sequence

  • Event announcement.
  • Why attend.
  • Speaker or facilitator credibility.
  • Social proof or relevant evidence.
  • Frequently asked questions.
  • Genuine capacity or timing reminder.
  • Final registration deadline.

Registration sequence

  • Immediate confirmation.
  • Preparation information.
  • Venue and travel details.
  • 7-day, 3-day and 1-day reminders where suitable.
  • Event-day reminder.
  • Thank-you or missed-event branch.
  • Next step and feedback.

6. The event is not the end of the workflow.

Attendance creates a decision point. An attendee, a no-show, someone who booked a call and someone who purchased should not receive the same follow-up. Automation should also create visibility for humans: high-intent actions such as payment, booked calls or important replies need a clear internal owner.

7. Report the event as both a campaign and a funnel.

A useful dashboard separates marketing activity, lifecycle progression, paid-media efficiency and event-level outcomes. That makes location and audience comparisons possible without collapsing everything into one headline number.

8. Design for workflow failure.

The most damaging automation errors are usually quiet: duplicated reminders, contacts not removed after registration, incorrect tags, missing internal notifications and people receiving the wrong follow-up. Test the complete lifecycle with dummy contacts before the campaign goes live.

Pre-launch checklist

  1. Test forms on mobile and desktop.
  2. Confirm the CRM record is created or updated correctly.
  3. Confirm event and location status.
  4. Verify removal from acquisition after registration.
  5. Check date, venue, links and reminder timing.
  6. Test internal notifications.
  7. Test attendance and no-show branches.
  8. Test payment and booked-call exits where relevant.
  9. Confirm reporting fields can be filtered by event and location.
  10. Review consent and communication preferences.
  11. Document who owns the workflow after launch.

9. Build once, improve every event.

The long-term objective is one event-marketing system that can be duplicated for future workshops, conferences or location-based events with minimal setup while preserving the same lifecycle definitions and reporting logic.

Build the process once. Improve it every event.
